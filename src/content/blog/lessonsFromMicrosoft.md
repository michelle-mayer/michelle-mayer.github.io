I joined Microsoft in 2022 as a software engineer. I've learned plenty of technical
things since then, but the lessons that changed how I work the most weren't in any
onboarding doc. They were about people: how we handle change, how we share the
load, and how we show up for each other.

These are the ones I keep coming back to.

## Your relatives will text you about all things Microsoft

Starting the list off on a humorous note, one of the funniest things about working for a company as big as Microsoft is the feedback from friends and family. Any issue with Outlook, payments, Windows login, etc are routed to me with excitement that they "know someone on the inside". When in reality, I am the size of an ant compared to the ~200k employees needed to maintain an ecosystem as large as Microsoft. I'm flattered that they think I can help. If they have a question about the niche internal app my team develops, I'm the girl! Otherwise, ummm have you tried the 1800 support number? :) It's also funny the shade they throw during outages. Like "did you know Teams is broken for all customers"? Yes in fact, I was aware the world is burning down and thank goodness I'm not the on-call dev for that team.

## Working at a diverse global company is awesome
A little personal context - I grew up in a small town in South Carolina where there was very little racial, ethnic, political, or religious diversity. Most people from my town are born and die within a 50 mile radius (todo: can I make this claim without a source?). However, Microsoft is a global company that makes products for a plethora of people with different backgrounds, identities, beliefs, and abilities. We also employ people from those different backgrounds, because-- how can you build a product for a diverse consumer base without having engineers, designers, etc who understand the unique desires and needs of certain groups of people.

One thing I think Microsoft (at least historically) does really well is its emphasis on accommodating disabilities. For example, large employee meetings almost always have an ESL interpreter signing for deaf employees. 

As an employee, there are numerous learning opportunities when surrounded by individuals with extremely different backgrounds than yourself. Educating yourself on different cultures allows you to show up better as a coworker and friend. For example, avoiding booking important team lunches during Ramadan. Or making sure events/potlucks have plenty of non-beef, vegetarian, and vegan food options for Hindu colleagues. It has helped me examine my own privileges, many that might have gone unnoticed I was surrounded by a more homogenous circle.

## Quality vs speed is a pendulum (along with many other tech trends)
One piece of wisdom that many of my mentors have passed along is how most business trends will eventually swing back and forth like pendulums. It may take years, but over time, there will be a correction back towards the other direction. For example, organization depth versus breadth. Post covid, Microsoft did tons of hiring, and this led to deeper org charts with more layers between low-level employees and the CEO. However, in the past few years, the company has been restructuring to reduce hierarchy, cutting lots of "middle management". The "count from Sataya" is often a verbiage myself and colleagues jokingly use to describe how "high up" we are. Though, it is more a reflection of organizational shape trends than actual power :)

Over time, company guidance swings back and forth, and employees have to be adept at prioritizing the latest guidance. One that I personally struggle navigating (especially in the **rapid** pace of the "AI Race") is engineering Quality vs Speed. It's somewhat funny (if not fickle) how quickly priorities change based on customer sentiment and competitive priorities. Only a little while ago, "quality" was the buzzword that leaders couldn't get enough of. Azure was perceived as unreliable compared to competing cloud providers, and high-value customers were threatening to take business elsewhere if outages kept occurring. Numerous teams and new products were created with the mission of encouraging engineering best-practices and enforcing org-wide standards -- safe deployments, secure-by-default, etc. There was so much scrutiny when making changes that I was worried about engineers feeling comfortable deploying **anything**. Now with AI, the pendulum has swung in the totally opposite direction. Management wants daily deployments, heck, multiple a day if you can! 🤪 There is less praise for writing high-quality reusable code. The focus is "speed speed speed" 🏃‍♀️. When I started at Microsoft, my org did "semester" feature planning (looking 6 months forward). It then moved to quarterly, and now almost all teams adhere to a squad model which forces features to be delivered in 6 week periods, even if the realistic scope of work is beyond that duration... ## Change is constant
I am someone who doesn't love change, which is probably a scary thing to admit in tech... 🫣 Whilst employees should try their best to be adaptable, it is human nature to feel unsettled when a beloved teammate leaves, a project you've greatly invested in gets shut down/outsourced, or there's a shakeup in your management chain (and therefore, a change in who is responsible for promoting you). From reorgs, to reprioritizations, to attrition-- you name it-- it feels like we're always "storming" [source]. In my first 4 years at Microsoft, I had 3 different managers and worked on 5 different products.

Pros:
- Broader network of coworkers
- Broader product/tech stack exposure (different customers, priorities, on-call experiences etc.)
- A bad project, manager, policy, etc probably won't stay long

Cons:
- Constant out-of-control change lowers employee comfort and causes them to develop "protective apathy"-- you learn not to get overly invested in connections with teammates and certain projects because things can change at a moment's notice.
- Too much churn prevents reaching "flow". For example, a manager needs to know you enough before they feel comfortable advocating for your promotion.

## Titles are **to a degree** meaningless
I'll ruffle a few traditional feathers with this one, but hear me out. From an outside perspective, your company-specific job title may affect people's perception of you on LinkedIn, but it doesn't tell the whole story. 

For example, most people outside of Microsoft don't know that each Software Engineer job title contains levels inside of it that only the manager and employee are aware of. You might have the same "title" as someone else, i.e. "Senior Software Engineer", but your specific numerical "level" is really what defines your compensation range and performance expectations.
