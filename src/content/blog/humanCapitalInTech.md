There is a message I keep hearing in tech: move faster. Use AI. Deliver more.
Don't let the company fall behind in the AI arms race.

I understand the urgency. I also keep wondering what happens when urgency stops
being a response to a particular challenge and becomes the permanent culture.

Somewhere in that shift, the things that make work feel human can start to look
expendable: a friendly greeting, time to get to know a teammate, a team lunch,
room to ask for help, a workday that actually ends.

Nobody has to announce that these things no longer matter. It can be enough to
make them feel like time we cannot afford.

**My concern is not that we are taking AI too seriously. It is that we risk
taking the people building with it less seriously.**

## Culture is not a nice-to-have for everyone

I have a higher baseline of neuroticism, and culture is something I need at work.
It was part of why I chose Microsoft.

That is not the opposite of ambition. Wanting to do excellent work and needing a
healthy environment in which to do it are not contradictory.

When I say culture, I do not just mean social events. I mean how people treat
each other when deadlines are tight, whether expectations are clear, whether
help is available, and whether it is safe to say that something is not working.

I am writing from my own perspective, not on behalf of my employer.

## Constant change has a human cost

Layoffs, performance improvement plans (PIPs), and reorganizations are different
things, but each can introduce uncertainty. A reorg can also break up
relationships and routines that took time to build.

When those changes keep arriving, I worry that people can move from a
collaboration mindset into a scarcity mindset:

- Is it safe to admit that I need help?
- Will sharing credit make my own contribution less visible?
- Should I spend time helping a teammate, or protecting my own position?
- Is taking time off going to look like a lack of commitment?

These are possible responses, not a description of every employee or every team.
But they are questions worth asking before treating an anxious workplace as a
high-performance one.

## Who are we designing this pace for?

Sometimes the ideal employee in this race seems to be an imaginary person:
always available, endlessly adaptable, unaffected by uncertainty, and able to
absorb more work without needing more recovery.

That is a narrow definition of who gets to thrive.

This is not about dividing people into "emotional" and "non-emotional," or
assuming that a neurotype determines someone's ability to succeed. Everyone has
emotions. People differ in temperament, support needs, responsibilities outside
work, and how they respond to stress.

We should not confuse being good at tolerating a particular environment with
being the only kind of person capable of excellent engineering.

If we want a range of people and perspectives building AI, we need working
conditions in which that range of people can succeed.

## Burnout is not a sustainable productivity strategy

I worry that some of what we celebrate as acceleration may be borrowing from
people's future capacity to work.

The [World Health Organization's overview of mental health at work](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-at-work)
identifies excessive workloads, low control, limited support, and job insecurity
as risks to mental health. It also explains that healthier working environments
can support retention, performance, and productivity.

That does not mean someone experiencing anxiety or illness is inherently less
capable. It means working conditions can make it harder for people to do their
best work, and organizations have a role in improving those conditions.

If the short-term gain is more output, but the longer-term result is exhaustion,
absence, or people leaving, are we measuring the full cost of that speed?

And even if there were no productivity benefit, people would still deserve a
healthy workplace. The business case matters, but it should not be the only case.

Work is not happening in a vacuum. People arrive with lives, responsibilities,

For broader context, WHO estimates that depression and anxiety account for
12 billion lost working days annually worldwide. That estimate covers the wider
economy; it is not a measure of tech burnout or evidence of an AI-specific effect.

The question I want to ask is what happens when workplace pressure compounds
the stress people are already carrying, rather than giving them a stable place
to contribute.

## Making room for people is part of the strategy

I am not arguing against accountability, ambitious goals, or learning new tools.
I am arguing against treating basic human needs as obstacles to those goals.

Some places to start:

- **Make priorities explicit.** If something new becomes urgent, say what can
  wait instead of quietly adding it to an already full workload.
- **Protect recovery time.** Do not make constant availability the unofficial
  requirement for being seen as committed.
- **Preserve connection.** Leave room for greetings, check-ins, and getting to
  know the people whose judgment and help you rely on.
- **Make connection inclusive.** Team bonding should not require after-hours
  attendance, extroversion, or sharing personal information.
- **Communicate through change.** Be clear about what is known, what is not, and
  when people can expect another update.
- **Reward collaboration.** Recognize mentoring, knowledge sharing, and helping
  teammates, not just the most visible individual output.
- **Make it safe to raise a concern.** Asking for help or questioning an
  unrealistic deadline should not be treated as a character flaw.

A team lunch cannot compensate for an impossible workload. Culture has to show
up in decisions, not just activities.

## The race is not an excuse to forget the people running it

We can be ambitious about AI without accepting that the price of admission is
exhaustion, emotional suppression, or a life organized entirely around work.

When we talk about human capital, I want us to remember the human part.

**The goal should not be to find the few people who can endure an unhealthy
pace. It should be to build an environment where different people can do
excellent work sustainably.**
