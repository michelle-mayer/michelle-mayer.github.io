## It's not me, it's we (ted talk credit?)
This piece of personal advice is for the people pleaser who often gets caught up analyzing what other people are thinking. If you've ever been re-reading a Teams message attempting to determine if the sender is frustrated with you-- read on 😅

I grew up in a "bless your heart" Southern culture where critiques are indirect and even enemies are greeted with a smile. There's pros and cons to this of course. In corporate America, the communication style is often more direct, and I struggled with receiving this at first, perceiving it as rude or abrasive.

Remind yourself that communication is a two way street. Just because someone used verbiage that would be angry coming from **you** doesn't mean that the sender intended it with that tone.     I'll use my mom as an example because I know she won't mind, haha. My mom is known for replying to my texts with "Ok." If the scenario was reveresed, this response from me or any of my friends would be passive aggressive-- trying to signal that you're frustrated by being brief and disengaged. From my mom's perspective, she's just acknowledging my message and replying in the shortest way possible (it might even be difficult for her to type or see the keyboard). 

A classic example is the "boomer ellipses". 


Working in a diverse environment, requires communication to be more explicit for the sake of alignment and clarity (read the Culture Map for a deep dive). The lack of shared cultural context means less safe assumptions can be made.

In general, the world would probably run smoother if we assumed best intent of others. Every response a person gives you is formed by **their** mood, personality, culture, et. Maybe someone was rude to you at work. Chances are, this says more about how **their** day is going than how they feel about **you**. It's human nature to think that people are always perceiving or treating us a certain way. In reality, someone sending you a rude email might mean that **their** mom is sick, or **they're** sleep deprived from a newborn, maybe they're super stressed about a deadline, maybe they have a headache, maybe someone was just rude to **them** and they're feeling emotionally heightened... You name it.

Of course, we can still have our preferences and try our best to lead by example of the culture we want at work.

On a less serious note, if someone is genuinely being unnecessarily rude or disrespectful but you don't want a confrontation, a great response is "Hey, are you doing okay? You seem stressed." Some might think this is passive aggressive, but IMO it can be a helpful tool to indirectly acknowledge that you've noticed their escalation in tone without explicitly saying "you're being rude to me".

## Constructive feedback usually means a person respects you and believes you're capable
Growing up, many of us are taught that no constructive feedback means we did an amazing job-- an A+ on the essay, no notes. 

However, building enterprise products is more complex than acing a multiple-choice test. First off, if we waited until every single possible "i" is dotted and "t" is crossed, we would take years to deliver and be obsolete in the market. 

In fact, a product or feature can be extremely successful while still having ideas for expansion, rework, or improvement. The same goes for interpersonal feedback. 

If a person sets time out of their busy day to give you some kind and actionable suggestions for improvement, it is a gift they are giving you. They respect you and **believe** that you have even more potential. It reminds me of dancing ballet growing up. I would notice the teachers often only giving specific corrections to the best in the class. It's counterintuitive- your name is called out to fix XYZ and you think "oh how embarrassing to be called out". But 99% of the time it means the teacher notices your talent and is specifically invested in **your** improvement.

Some of my favorite collaborators in my career have been the individuals who give me direct tips, like they would to any other engineer. Being young, friendly, and female in the tech space, I would notice individuals being less willing to give me direct feedback. Maybe because I seem sensitive or not receptive, or it's just bias. 


## Your "yes" is a commitment, so size it like one
A quick favor rarely stays quick. In tech, someone usually plans around your
answer: a dependency, a launch date, an on-call swap. Treat every yes like an
estimate. "Yes, by Thursday" or "Yes, if this other task can wait" is still a yes,
and it's one you can keep.

## Kind code reviews still say what's wrong
Approving a pull request you have doubts about can feel nicer than leaving a
blocking comment. It isn't. The bug ships anyway, and now it's in production.
Clear, specific feedback, with a suggested fix where you can, respects the
author's time more than a polite approval.

## Disagree in writing, and early
If speaking up in a meeting feels confrontational, leave your concern in the
design doc or the pull request thread instead. Writing gives you time to choose
your words, and your comment lands before a decision hardens, while changing
course is still cheap.

## Raising a blocker isn't throwing anyone under the bus
People pleasers tend to absorb problems quietly so no one looks bad. But a risk
raised early is a planning detail; the same risk raised the day before a deadline
is a crisis. Flagging it is part of the job, not a betrayal.

## Being everyone's go-to isn't the same as having impact
Answering every question and picking up every loose end feels good, and teams
need people who do it. But much of that work is invisible at review time. Make
sure it's visible, and that it isn't crowding out the work you're actually
measured on.
